Patterns / Change over time

The difference between adding
and compounding.

Linear growth adds the same amount each period. Compound growth applies the same rate to a changing base—so the amount added grows too.

The distinction is small at first and consequential over time. This example starts both systems at 100 units so you can see exactly where the gap comes from.

Same starting point. Different trajectories.

Change the rate to inspect how the two rules diverge over ten periods.

Hypothetical data

At 10%, compounding reaches 259.4 units after ten periods. Linear growth reaches 200.0. The 59.4-unit difference comes entirely from applying the rate to the accumulated gains.

The rate stays constant; the increment does not. Each period's gain becomes part of the next period's base. The example above uses 10% to make that feedback visible.

What this establishes

The widening gap follows directly from the two recurrence rules. It does not need an assumed change in the growth rate.

Where the model stops

These are idealized calculations, not observations or forecasts. Real processes can face changing rates, resource limits, delays and shocks. A good mathematical fit alone does not establish a causal explanation.
